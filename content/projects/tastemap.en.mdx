Taste Map is an iOS app I designed, built and shipped to the App Store on my own: a private map of
every place you have eaten at and every place you want to try. It's written in SwiftUI and
SwiftData with no backend at all, and a small Next.js site carries the pages the App Store requires.

## Context

I kept a list of restaurants in a notes app for years, and it failed in two ways. Star ratings
drifted: a four-star place from last spring meant nothing next to a four-star place from last week.
And the list couldn't answer the one question I actually asked it, which was where to eat tonight.

So the app was built around those two jobs. Ranking replaces rating, so every place has a position
relative to the others. A *Decide* flow turns the list into a recommendation. Everything else (the
map, the visit log, the stats) exists to feed those two.

Two constraints came from the start. The app had to work **without an account or a server**, since
a food diary is personal and I didn't want to run infrastructure for it. And it had to ship in
**English and Turkish** with both reading naturally, not one translated from the other.

## What I built

- **Head-to-head ranking.** A new place is never given a score. The app asks "which was better?"
  against places you've already ranked, and a binary search places it in about log₂ n questions,
  so 14 ranked places take four questions. A "too close to call" answer ends the session early,
  and the ranks stay dense (1…n) through every insert and delete.
- **A map that shows what you've been to.** Pins are photo stickers with curved cuisine labels.
  They scale with zoom, group into clusters and expand into a place card in place. Cafés and
  restaurants from Apple Maps can be saved with one tap, and filter chips narrow the map by
  status, list, cuisine or price.
- **Decide, with on-device notes.** A few quick questions (how far, who's coming, which cuisine)
  narrow the list to a handful of picks. Where Apple Intelligence is available, each pick comes
  with a short "why this fits" note generated on the device with the Foundation Models framework.
  Where it isn't, the picks simply show without a note.

## Decisions and trade-offs

**Native components first.** The rule in the codebase is to reach for `List`, `Form`, `Menu`,
swipe actions and Liquid Glass before drawing anything custom, and to fix a visual by choosing the
right system style rather than painting over it. Custom work went where it earned its place: the
map pins and the ranking flow. Everything else gets accessibility, Dynamic Type and new OS styling
from the system.

**SwiftData, no server.** Places, visits and lists are SwiftData models on the device. That made
privacy simple to explain, with no sign-up and nothing leaving the phone unless you export or share
it, and it cut out a whole layer of infrastructure. The cost is that every cross-device feature
has to be earned rather than assumed. I accepted that trade.

**Pure logic outside the views.** Ranking, filtering, the Decide engine, deep-link parsing and
stats are plain structs and enums, so they can be tested without a model container. 210 Swift
Testing checks cover that layer. Anything that touches SwiftData gets a throwaway in-memory
container, never the real store.

**One codebase, one platform abstraction.** Shared code never reaches UIKit directly. A small
`Platform.swift` layer wraps images, colours, sheet sizing and list styles, so the same screens
compile for iOS and macOS. Genuinely iOS-only features such as alternate icons and Instagram
Stories sit behind `#if os(iOS)`.

**Links that don't leak.** A shared list link carries the list in the URL fragment. The fragment
never reaches the server, and the landing page doesn't read it, so a link can be opened by someone
without the app and nobody's list is ever logged.

## Challenges

**Turkish that reads like Turkish.** Getting strings translated was the easy part; getting them to
sound right was not. Several passes went into tone alone: Decide's notes address the user as
*sen*, plurals use inflection rules instead of string concatenation, and ternaries between string
literals are banned because they silently skip localization.

**Holding a feature back.** I built optional private iCloud sync, including chunked, checksum-verified
media transfer, and then hid it before release. Local tests passed, but I hadn't verified
two-device transfer on real hardware, and I wasn't willing to ship "sync" on a guess. It cost
something later: a store that had once been mirrored refused a schema migration at launch. The fix
copies the store aside, clears CloudKit's bookkeeping tables and reopens it without touching a
single record.

**Shipping is its own project.** Two dozen TestFlight builds, App Store screenshots for three device
sizes, and listing copy and legal pages in two languages took as much care as the features did.
That work is why the website exists, and why the pages describe only what the shipped build
actually does.

**AI-assisted, still reviewed.** I worked with coding agents throughout, guided by a repository
guide that sets the conventions: design tokens only, a preview for every view, and verification on
the simulator before a change counts as done. The agents made me faster. Holding every change to
those rules is what kept the codebase mine.
